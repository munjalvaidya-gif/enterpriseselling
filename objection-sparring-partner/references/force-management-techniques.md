# Force Management Techniques — Objection Response Modes

Three techniques this tool explicitly models and grades against. Each entry has: what it is, when it fits, a strong example, a weak counterexample that sounds fine but isn't, and how the in-character persona should react differently to each during Step 3's roleplay.

## 1. Command of the Message

**What it is:** Controlling the conversation by leading with quantifiable business value and real differentiation tied to *this specific* buyer's situation — instead of reacting to an objection at the feature level. The seller sets the frame; they don't just respond inside the frame the buyer handed them.

**Fits:** Objections about Metrics, Decision Criteria, or Identify Pain — anywhere the buyer is implicitly asking "why does this matter to us."

**Strong example** (buyer: "We already have a tool that does most of this"):
> "Most of what, specifically — the reporting layer, or the underlying data pipeline? Because if it's the pipeline, that's usually where the real cost is hiding. What's it costing your team in engineering hours to keep that running today?"

**Weak counterexample** (sounds fine, loses ground):
> "Sure, but our tool has more integrations and a better UI, so it's still worth a look."

The weak version defends on features and invites a feature-by-feature comparison the seller probably can't win from a cold start. The strong version redirects to a cost the buyer hasn't quantified yet, which only the seller's framing supplied.

**How the persona should react:** Genuine engagement — a real follow-up question, a slightly more specific admission ("actually, it's more than I'd like to spend on that, yeah") — to a strong Command-of-the-Message response. Flat, polite non-commitment ("noted, we'll factor that in") to a feature-level defense; the persona shouldn't be rude about it, just unmoved.

## 2. Negative Reverse Selling

**What it is:** When a buyer voices doubt or hesitation, the seller agrees with it — or even amplifies it — instead of arguing against it. Counterintuitively, this lowers the buyer's defenses (there's nothing left to push back against) and draws out the real objection underneath the polite one.

**Fits:** Vague, deflecting, or overly polite objections — "not sure this is a priority," "we're fine for now," "maybe next quarter" — where the real concern is being hidden behind a soft no.

**Strong example** (buyer: "Honestly, I'm not sure this is a priority for us right now"):
> "That's fair, it might not be. Can I ask what would actually have to change for it to become one?"

**Weak counterexample:**
> "I understand, but I really think once you see the ROI you'll agree this should be a priority."

The weak version argues against the stated position, which makes the buyer defend it harder. The strong version agrees, removing the need to defend anything, and asks a question that surfaces the real blocker.

**How the persona should react:** When the seller uses this correctly, the persona should give a more candid, specific answer than their opening line — that's the mechanism working. When the seller argues past the doubt instead, the persona should hold the polite deflection and not open up further; repeating a generic reassurance is a miss, and the debrief should call it out as one.

**In-session testing:** occasionally the persona should voice a soft, deflecting doubt on its own (not only in response to a seller pitch) specifically to create the opening for this technique — see SKILL.md Step 3. Track whether the seller takes it.

## 3. Compelling Event / Cost of Inaction

**What it is:** Anchoring the case to a specific, real trigger or deadline (a compliance date, a renewal, a competitive threat, a cost the buyer is already feeling) and quantifying what *not* acting costs — rather than a generic "here's the ROI of our product" pitch that could apply to any prospect at any time.

**Fits:** Objections about timing, urgency, or "why now" — "let's revisit this next quarter," "we're not in a rush on this."

**Strong example** (buyer: "There's no rush on this from our end"):
> "What happens on your end if this is still unresolved by [specific event the account is known to have — a renewal, a compliance deadline, a stated initiative]? I ask because that's usually what turns 'no rush' into a hard deadline for other teams in your position."

**Weak counterexample:**
> "Companies that adopt solutions like ours typically see a 30% improvement within six months, so the sooner you start the sooner you see that."

The weak version is a generic industry stat untethered to anything specific about this account — it could be said to anyone. The strong version ties urgency to something real and specific to this buyer's situation.

**How the persona should react:** Real engagement with a concrete event should get real engagement back ("...actually, yeah, that's a fair point given [X]"). A generic ROI stat with no anchor to anything specific about the account should leave the persona unconvinced — press for specifics ("compared to what, exactly, and by when does that matter to us") rather than accepting the number at face value.
